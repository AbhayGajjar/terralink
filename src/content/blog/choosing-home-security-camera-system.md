Buying a security camera system can feel like comparing spec sheets in a foreign language: 4K, 8MP, PoE, NVR, WDR, IR range, H.265. The good news is that a handful of decisions matter far more than the rest. Get those right and almost any reputable system will serve you well.

This guide covers what to think about, in the order we think about it when we design a system for an Adelaide home.

## Start with what you want to capture

Before you look at a single camera, list the specific things you want footage of. For example:

- **Faces** of people at the front door.
- **Number plates** of cars entering the driveway.
- **Movement** through the side gate or back entry.
- **The shed or garage**, where tools and bikes are kept.
- **Parcels** being delivered or taken.

Each of these needs a camera at a particular height, angle and distance. "Cover the backyard" usually produces footage of the tops of heads; "capture a face at the side gate" produces footage you can use.

## Wired or Wi-Fi?

### Wired (PoE) cameras with an NVR

Each camera connects to a **network video recorder (NVR)** with a single network cable that carries both power and video. This is called **PoE (power over ethernet)**. The NVR records to a hard drive and connects to your router for phone viewing.

- **Pros:** very reliable, no batteries to charge, no monthly fees, recording continues even if the internet drops, and the best image quality.
- **Cons:** needs cabling, usually through the roof space, so installation takes longer.

### Wi-Fi and battery cameras

These connect over your home Wi-Fi and are often battery-powered or plugged into a nearby power point.

- **Pros:** quick to install, good for renters or a single extra camera.
- **Cons:** depend on strong Wi-Fi, batteries need charging, recording may only start after motion is detected (so the start of an event can be missed), and many store footage in the cloud for a monthly fee.

**Our view:** for whole-property coverage, wired is worth it. Wi-Fi cameras work well as an add-on, such as for a granny flat or a single spot that's hard to cable.

## Resolution: more pixels isn't everything

Camera resolution is usually quoted in megapixels: 4MP, 6MP, 8MP (often sold as "4K"). Higher resolution means more detail, so you can zoom in further on recorded footage. But:

- A 4K camera with a poor sensor can look worse at night than a good 4MP camera.
- Higher resolution means bigger files, so you need more storage for the same number of days.
- **Lens and placement** matter as much as resolution. A wide-angle lens covering a whole yard spreads its pixels thinly; a narrower or varifocal lens aimed at a gate puts them where you need them.

For most homes, good-quality 4MP to 8MP cameras are the sweet spot.

## Night vision and difficult lighting

- **Infrared (IR) night vision** gives black-and-white footage in darkness. Check the rated IR range, and remember that IR reflects off nearby walls, eaves and spider webs, which washes out the image.
- **Colour night vision** cameras use sensitive sensors and sometimes a small white light to produce colour footage in low light. Colour can be useful for identifying clothing and cars.
- **WDR (wide dynamic range)** helps when a camera sees bright and dark areas at once, such as a shaded porch looking out to a sunny street. It's important for west-facing cameras in Adelaide's harsh afternoon light.

## Storage: how many days do you need?

On an NVR system, footage records to a hard drive until it's full, then the oldest footage is overwritten. How long that takes depends on:

- the number of cameras;
- resolution and compression (H.265 uses less space than H.264);
- whether recording is continuous or only when there's motion; and
- the hard drive size.

Most homes aim for **two to four weeks** of footage. That gives you time to notice an incident and save the footage. Whatever system you choose, **save important clips straight away**.

Cloud storage, common on Wi-Fi cameras, keeps footage off-site, which helps if the recorder is stolen, but usually costs a monthly fee per camera. Placing the NVR out of sight, such as in a cupboard, achieves much of the same protection.

## Smart detection and alerts

Modern cameras can tell the difference between people, vehicles and other movement. That means fewer false alerts from trees, cats and passing headlights. Combined with **motion zones** (only alert for movement in the driveway, not the street), smart detection makes phone notifications useful rather than annoying.

## Where to put cameras

- **Front door:** around head height or slightly above, angled to see faces rather than hats. A video doorbell is a good complement.
- **Driveway:** positioned to see number plates as cars enter, not just from above.
- **Side gates and rear entries:** this is where most break-ins start.
- **Shed or garage:** covering the door.
- **Height:** high enough to be hard to tamper with (around 2.5–3 metres) but not so high you only see the tops of heads.
- **Avoid:** pointing straight at the sun, at bright lights or at reflective surfaces, and mounting directly under eaves where IR bounces back.

## Power and backup

Cameras and NVRs need power. A small **UPS (battery backup)** keeps the system running through short outages, which is useful during Adelaide's summer storms and heatwaves. If your internet goes down, wired systems keep recording locally; you just can't view them remotely until it's back.

## Privacy and the law in South Australia

In South Australia, the *Surveillance Devices Act 2016 (SA)* regulates cameras and listening devices. As a general guide:

- point cameras at your **own property**;
- avoid capturing **neighbours' private areas**, such as windows and backyards, and use the privacy masking feature to block them out if needed;
- be very careful with **audio recording**, which can be an offence when it captures private conversations without consent; and
- businesses have extra considerations, including signage, staff notification and how footage is stored and accessed.

## Budget: what affects the price

The main factors are the number and quality of cameras, recorder size and storage, how far the cables run, the type of roof and building height, and extras such as doorbells or monitors. A cheaper camera that captures a usable face is worth more than an expensive one in the wrong place, so put your budget into **good placement and coverage of entry points** first.

## DIY or professional installation?

DIY can work for a couple of Wi-Fi cameras. For a wired, whole-property system, professional installation gets you concealed cabling, sealed roof and wall penetrations, correct angles and a properly configured recorder, plus someone to call if something stops working.
