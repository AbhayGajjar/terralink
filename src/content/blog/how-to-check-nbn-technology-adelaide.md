Knowing your nbn technology helps you choose the right plan, understand why your connection behaves the way it does, and find out whether you can upgrade to fibre. It also helps any technician you call, because the right fix for a slow FTTN line is very different from the right fix for a patchy FTTP home.

Here are three ways to find out, from the most reliable to the quickest, plus what each result means.

## Method 1: use nbn's address checker (most reliable)

The address checker on the official nbn website is the definitive source.

1. Go to the nbn website and find the **check your address** tool, usually on the home page.
2. Type your street address and select it from the list. Include your unit number if you're in an apartment or unit.
3. Read the result. It shows the **technology type** serving your premises, whether the premises is ready to connect, and, for FTTN and FTTC addresses, whether a **fibre upgrade** is available.

If your address doesn't appear, try variations such as "Unit 2" or "2/" and check the suburb spelling. For brand-new homes, the address may not be listed until it's been registered with nbn.

## Method 2: look at the equipment in your home

Your equipment usually gives the technology away:

| What you see | Likely technology |
| --- | --- |
| A white **nbn connection box** on an inside wall with a thin fibre cable, plus a small grey or white **utility box** outside | **FTTP** |
| A small **nbn connection device** (about the size of a paperback) plugged into the **phone socket**, with your modem plugged into it | **FTTC** |
| An **nbn connection box** connected to a **coaxial (TV-style) wall outlet** | **HFC** |
| Your **modem plugged straight into the phone socket**, with no nbn box | **FTTN** (or FTTB in an apartment block) |
| An **antenna** on the roof pointing to a tower, with a box inside | **Fixed Wireless** |
| A **satellite dish** on the roof | **Satellite** |

This is a good quick check, but older equipment can be misleading, for example if a previous owner had a different service. Confirm with the address checker.

## Method 3: ask your internet provider

Your provider knows exactly which technology your service runs on. It's often shown in your online account or on your bill, or you can ask their support team. They can also tell you your line's actual maximum speed, which is useful on FTTN and FTTC.

## What your result means

### FTTP

You have the best fixed-line technology. If your internet is slow or patchy, the cause is usually your router, Wi-Fi coverage or where the connection box is. See [FTTP installation in Adelaide](/fttp-installation-adelaide).

### FTTN

Your speed depends on your distance from the node and the condition of the copper, inside and outside your home. Two things to do:

- Check whether an **FTTP upgrade** is available at your address. If it is, read [FTTP upgrade explained](/blog/fttp-upgrade-explained-eligibility).
- If speeds are poor, have the **internal wiring** checked. Extension sockets are a common culprit. See [FTTN installation and wiring](/services/fttn-installation-adelaide).

### FTTC

You're on a better copper-based technology than FTTN, and many FTTC addresses can now upgrade to fibre. Keep the nbn connection device plugged in and switched on at all times, because it powers equipment in the street.

### HFC

HFC supports fast plans. If you have problems, look for old pay-TV splitters or damaged coaxial cables inside the house. See [FTTC and HFC installation](/services/fttc-hfc-installation-adelaide).

### Fixed Wireless or Satellite

These serve properties where a fixed line isn't practical, including some semi-rural parts of the Adelaide Hills. nbn installs the antenna or dish through your provider; a local technician can help with cabling inside and around the property.

### "Not nbn" or another network

Some new estates are served by a fibre network that isn't owned by nbn. In that case you'll need a provider that sells plans on that network. The estate developer or your builder can tell you which network serves your lot.

## Checking a home you're about to buy or rent

Internet matters more than ever when choosing a home, especially if you work from home. Before you sign:

- **Run the address through nbn's checker** as soon as you have the exact address. Listings rarely mention the nbn technology, and when they do it isn't always accurate.
- **Look for the equipment at the inspection.** A connection box on an inside wall, a device on the phone socket or a coax wall outlet tells you a lot. Note where it is: a box in the garage may mean you'll want cabling to a central spot.
- **Ask about upgrades.** If the result is FTTN or FTTC with an upgrade available, factor that in. Renters will need the owner's permission to upgrade.
- **Ask the current occupants** how reliable the connection has been, if you get the chance.

## When the result doesn't match what you see

Sometimes the address checker and the equipment in the house don't seem to agree. Common reasons:

- **An upgrade is in progress**, so the checker shows the new technology before the equipment has been installed.
- **Leftover equipment** from a previous service or a different network, such as an old pay-TV outlet that isn't used for nbn.
- **The wrong address entry**, which is common with units, rear dwellings and corner blocks. Try each variation of the address.
- **A new home not yet registered** with nbn, so it shows as unavailable even though fibre runs past it.

If you're still unsure, your provider can confirm the technology linked to your service.

## Why does it vary so much across Adelaide?

nbn used different technologies in different areas depending on the existing infrastructure, cost and timing. That's why one Adelaide suburb can be mostly FTTN while a newer estate nearby is all fibre, and why the technology can change from one street to the next.
