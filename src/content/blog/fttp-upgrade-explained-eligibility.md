If your home is on FTTN or FTTC, you may have seen ads or emails about upgrading to "full fibre". For many households it's one of the best improvements they can make to their internet, and for eligible homes the standard installation generally doesn't cost extra. But there are conditions, and a few practical hurdles that can slow things down.

This guide explains how the upgrade works, who's eligible, and how to make sure it goes smoothly.

> **Note:** nbn's upgrade rules, eligible plans and timeframes change from time to time. Always confirm the current details with nbn or your provider before ordering. <!-- VERIFY: all program details in this article against the current nbn website before publishing. -->

## What is the FTTP upgrade?

nbn has been replacing the copper part of eligible FTTN and FTTC connections with fibre, so the premises becomes FTTP. Rather than upgrading every home automatically, nbn has generally run it as an **on-demand** program: the upgrade happens when an eligible household orders a qualifying plan through a participating provider. nbn has referred to this as its fibre upgrade or "Fibre Connect" program.

After the upgrade, your home has the same equipment as any other FTTP home: a utility box outside and an nbn connection box inside.

## Who is eligible?

Eligibility depends on your **address**, not on you. In general:

- Your premises must currently be **FTTN or FTTC**.
- nbn must have extended fibre along your street. This has been rolled out in stages.
- You need to order an **eligible plan** with a **participating provider**. Historically this has meant a higher-speed plan. <!-- VERIFY: current plan requirement. -->

**How to check:** enter your address in the address checker on the nbn website. If an upgrade is available, it will say so. You can also ask your provider. If you're not eligible yet, check again in a few months.

### Renters, units and strata

- **Renters** need the property owner's permission, because the upgrade involves installing equipment and drilling through walls.
- **Units and apartments** may need approval from the strata or body corporate, and some multi-dwelling buildings are handled differently from houses. <!-- VERIFY: nbn rules for multi-dwelling units. -->

## What does it cost?

For eligible premises, nbn has generally provided the **standard installation at no cost**, provided you order a qualifying plan. Things to check:

- **Plan conditions.** You may need to stay on a qualifying plan for a minimum period. <!-- VERIFY -->
- **Non-standard installations.** Unusual situations may involve extra charges; your provider will tell you. <!-- VERIFY -->
- **Work on your property** that isn't part of nbn's installation, such as repairing a blocked conduit, running data cabling to another room or setting up mesh Wi-Fi, is separate and is done by a contractor of your choice.

## How the upgrade works, step by step

1. **Check eligibility** using nbn's address checker.
2. **Order** a qualifying plan with a participating provider and ask for the fibre upgrade.
3. **External work.** nbn runs fibre from the street to your home, usually through the existing underground lead-in conduit or from a pole, and installs the utility box on the outside wall. You may not need to be home for this part.
4. **Internal installation.** A technician installs the nbn connection box inside, near a power point, and connects and tests it. An adult must be home.
5. **Switch-over.** Your provider moves your service from copper to fibre. Your old FTTN modem may need replacing or reconfiguring, because FTTP connects via ethernet rather than the phone socket.
6. **Set up your home network.** Connect the router, check speeds on a wired device, and sort out Wi-Fi coverage.

## How long does it take?

From order to activation, it's often several weeks, depending on nbn's scheduling, your street and whether any problems are found along the way. <!-- VERIFY: typical timeframe. --> The internal installation appointment usually takes a few hours.

## What can delay an upgrade?

### Blocked or damaged conduit

The fibre is usually pulled through the same underground conduit as your old phone line. In established Adelaide suburbs, that conduit can be decades old and crushed by tree roots, broken by landscaping or separated by reactive clay soil. If nbn can't get the fibre through, the part of the conduit on your property is your responsibility to fix before the upgrade can continue.

Signs your conduit might be a problem: you've had recurring phone-line faults, there's been major landscaping or paving over the line's path, or a large tree sits between the pit and the house.

### Access and approvals

Locked gates, dogs, no adult home, or missing landlord or strata approval can all cause a missed appointment and a long wait for another.

### No power where the box should go

The nbn connection box needs a power point. If there isn't one where you want it, have an electrician install one beforehand.

## Is the upgrade worth it?

For most households on FTTN, yes, especially if:

- your current speeds are well below your plan, or drop out in the evening or after rain;
- several people stream, game or work from home at the same time;
- you want the option of faster plans in the future; or
- you're planning to sell or rent the property.

If your FTTN connection is close to the node and does everything you need, there's no rush. But because eligibility and terms can change, it's worth checking now.

## Does the upgrade change my provider or my phone number?

The upgrade is a change to the nbn connection, not to your provider. You can generally stay with your current provider if it offers the upgrade, or move to one that does. If you have a home phone service that runs through your modem, ask your provider how it will be moved across. Most keep the same number, but check before the switch-over day, particularly if you rely on the landline.

You should also check any contract terms with your current provider before switching plans, because changing to an eligible plan may count as a new contract.

## After the upgrade

- Old phone sockets are no longer used for internet.
- Make sure monitored alarms and medical alerts have been moved across.
- FTTP needs power; consider a small UPS for the connection box and router if you rely on the internet during outages.
- Use the opportunity to put your router somewhere central, and add data points where you need reliable wired connections.
