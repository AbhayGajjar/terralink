When you look up your address on the nbn website, you'll see one of a handful of acronyms: FTTP, FTTN, FTTC, HFC and, for some properties, FTTB, Fixed Wireless or Satellite. That label tells you a lot about the speeds you can get, how reliable your connection is likely to be, and what can go wrong.

This guide explains the four main fixed-line technologies in plain English, compares them side by side, and covers what it all means if you live in Adelaide.

## The short answer

- **FTTP (Fibre to the Premises)** is the best: fibre all the way into your home.
- **HFC (Hybrid Fibre Coaxial)** is a close second for speed, using the old pay-TV cable network.
- **FTTC (Fibre to the Curb)** brings fibre to the pit outside your property, with a short copper run to the house.
- **FTTN (Fibre to the Node)** brings fibre to a cabinet in your street, with a longer copper run. It's the most limited.

The big difference is **how much of the path to your home is copper**. Copper slows down over distance and deteriorates with age; fibre doesn't.

## FTTP: Fibre to the Premises

With FTTP, an optical fibre cable runs from nbn's network in the street to a utility box (the premises connection device) on an outside wall of your home. Inside, an nbn connection box converts the light signal, and your router plugs into it with an ethernet cable.

**Strengths**

- Supports the fastest residential plans available. <!-- VERIFY: current nbn speed tiers available on FTTP (e.g. up to ~1 Gbps). -->
- Speed doesn't drop with distance, so your neighbour 400 metres further away gets the same performance.
- Very reliable: no corroded copper joints and far less affected by wet weather.

**Things to know**

- The connection box needs power, so if the power goes out the connection stops unless you have battery backup.
- Where the box is installed matters for Wi-Fi coverage. See [what to expect on your FTTP installation day](/blog/what-to-expect-fttp-installation-day).

## HFC: Hybrid Fibre Coaxial

HFC reuses the coaxial cable network originally built for pay TV. Fibre runs to a node in the area and coaxial cable runs from there to your home, ending at a wall outlet. An nbn connection box connects to that outlet and your router connects to the box.

**Strengths**

- Can support very fast plans, close to FTTP on paper. <!-- VERIFY: HFC speed tier eligibility. -->
- Generally reliable once the in-home cabling is right.

**Things to know**

- HFC capacity is shared by homes in the same area, so heavy use can affect performance at busy times.
- Old pay-TV splitters, amplifiers and damaged coax inside the house are a common cause of problems. Our [FTTC and HFC service](/services/fttc-hfc-installation-adelaide) deals with exactly this.

## FTTC: Fibre to the Curb

With FTTC, fibre runs to a small distribution point unit in the pit near your property boundary. The existing copper phone line covers the last stretch, often only tens of metres, to your main phone socket. Inside, an nbn connection device (NCD) plugs into the socket and your modem plugs into the NCD.

**Strengths**

- The copper run is much shorter than FTTN, so speeds are generally better and more consistent.
- Many FTTC premises are now eligible to [upgrade to FTTP](/fttp-upgrade-adelaide). <!-- VERIFY: FTTC eligibility for nbn fibre upgrades. -->

**Things to know**

- The NCD supplies power to the equipment in the pit. If it's unplugged or switched off at the wall, your connection drops out.
- It's still copper, so old internal wiring and extension sockets can cause faults.

## FTTN: Fibre to the Node

With FTTN, fibre runs to a street cabinet (the node), which may be hundreds of metres from your home. The rest of the journey uses the existing copper phone network. Your modem plugs straight into the phone socket.

**Strengths**

- If you live close to the node and your copper is in good condition, FTTN can be perfectly adequate for everyday use.

**Things to know**

- Speed drops as the copper gets longer. Two homes in the same street can get very different speeds.
- FTTN generally can't support the fastest plans. <!-- VERIFY: FTTN maximum plan eligibility. -->
- Old copper, water in pits and internal extension wiring cause drop-outs and slow speeds. Fixing the wiring inside your home is often the most cost-effective improvement; see [FTTN installation and wiring](/services/fttn-installation-adelaide).

## Side-by-side comparison

| | FTTP | HFC | FTTC | FTTN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fibre goes to | Your home | A node in your area | The pit outside | A street cabinet |
| Last section | Fibre | Coaxial cable | Short copper run | Longer copper run |
| Fastest plans | Yes | Yes | Mid-range | Limited |
| Affected by distance | No | Not by distance, but shared capacity | Slightly | Yes, significantly |
| Main in-home issues | Box location, Wi-Fi coverage | Splitters, old coax | NCD power, extension wiring | Extension wiring, old sockets |
| Can upgrade to FTTP | Already fibre | Not generally | Many premises eligible | Many premises eligible |

## Which technologies are common in Adelaide?

Adelaide has all four. Very broadly:

- **Newer estates**, especially in the outer north and south, are usually fibre.
- **Established suburbs** are commonly served by FTTN or FTTC, with HFC in some areas.
- **Rural and semi-rural properties**, for example parts of the Adelaide Hills, may use fixed wireless or satellite.

The technology can change from one street to the next, so don't assume your neighbour's matches yours. Our guide on [how to check which nbn technology your address has](/blog/how-to-check-nbn-technology-adelaide) shows how to find out in a couple of minutes.

## Can you change your nbn technology?

Your technology is set by nbn for your address, and you can't simply choose a different one. The main exception is the **FTTP upgrade**: nbn has been extending fibre to many FTTN and FTTC premises, and eligible households can usually upgrade through their provider. <!-- VERIFY: nbn Fibre Connect program details. --> Read [FTTP upgrade explained](/blog/fttp-upgrade-explained-eligibility) to see how it works and whether it's worth it.

## What your technology means when choosing a plan

1. **Don't pay for speed your line can't deliver.** On FTTN in particular, check your line's actual maximum speed with your provider before choosing a premium plan.
2. **FTTP and HFC can make use of faster plans**, but your Wi-Fi and devices also need to keep up. A slow router or a box in the garage can bottleneck a fast plan.
3. **Wired beats wireless** for work computers, TVs and consoles on any technology.
